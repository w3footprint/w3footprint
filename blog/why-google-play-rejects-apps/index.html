Why Google Play rejects apps: 7 common reasons and how to fix them

Most Google Play rejections come from the same handful of issues. Here are the seven we see most often, and what to change before you resubmit.

Android29 October 20263 min readBy the W3Footprint team

In this article

  1. 1. Missing or weak privacy policy
  2. 2. Data safety form that does not match the app
  3. 3. Target API level too old
  4. 4. Permissions you do not need
  5. 5. Crashes and broken flows
  6. 6. Misleading listing
  7. 7. Spam, copied or minimum-functionality apps
  8. What to do after a rejection

A rejection email from Google Play is rarely about your code quality. It is usually about policy, paperwork or a mismatch between what the app does and what the listing says. These are the seven reasons we see most often.

1. Missing or weak privacy policy

If your app collects any personal data, including an email address, a device identifier or location, you need a privacy policy at a public URL, linked in the Play Console and, ideally, inside the app. It must describe what you really collect and why. A copied template that mentions things your app does not do is a problem too.

2. Data safety form that does not match the app

The Data safety section is a promise. If you declare that no data is collected but the app includes an analytics or ads library, review will notice. List every SDK, check what each one collects, and answer the form to match.

3. Target API level too old

Google raises the minimum target API level every year. New apps and updates that target an old level are blocked at upload or in review. Check the current requirement before you build, not after.

4. Permissions you do not need

Requesting location in the background, SMS, call logs or broad file access without a clear feature behind it is a fast way to get rejected. Ask only for what the feature needs, at the moment it is needed, and explain why on the permission prompt.

5. Crashes and broken flows

Reviewers open the app on real devices. A crash on first launch, a login that needs an account they cannot create, or a button that does nothing will get you rejected. If the app needs a login, give review a working test account in the access instructions.

6. Misleading listing

Screenshots that show features the app does not have, a title stuffed with keywords, or an icon that imitates another brand all count as metadata violations. Describe what the app does and show real screens.

7. Spam, copied or minimum-functionality apps

Apps that are a thin wrapper around a website, near-duplicates of other apps, or a single screen with ads can be rejected as low value. This catches many prototypes made in no-code and AI app builders. The fix is real functionality, offline behaviour where it makes sense, and a clear reason to install instead of using the web page.

What to do after a rejection

Read the policy link in the email, fix the exact issue, and reply in the Console with what you changed. Do not resubmit unchanged. If the message is unclear, appeal once with specifics. Repeated unchanged resubmissions can slow things down.
